\sectionline{Methodology}
{\small
\textbf{Data.} One row per driver per race since 2018, built three times: before the weekend, after practice and after qualifying. A feature is only filled when it really exists at that moment (no future information); missing information gets an availability flag of 0.

\textbf{Models.} B0: how often each grid slot finished where (baseline). B1: a rank-ordered logistic model. B2: gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM). M1: a Kalman filter that tracks each car's and driver's strength race by race. M2: a small neural network (PyTorch, CPU) with monotone constraints. M3: a race simulator. Each turns a strength score per driver into finishing-order probabilities with Plackett--Luce sampling (20\,000 simulated races).

\textbf{Blending and calibration.} Models are blended with weights fitted only on earlier races; probabilities can be tempered so that ``20\%'' really happens about 20\% of the time.

\textbf{Testing.} Walk-forward backtests on 2024--2025 (every race predicted using only earlier races). The 2026 season is a locked final test, evaluated once after the last race. Targets: \VAR{targets.win_logloss} (win) and \VAR{targets.podium_logloss} (podium) log loss, the best open-source model found.

\textbf{Not fooling ourselves.} A change is kept only if it is better on 2025 with a 95\% race-level bootstrap interval below zero, still better after a ``deflated'' correction for how many settings were tried (the best of 30 random tries looks good by luck), and better on the median of 7 combinatorial cross-validation paths over 2018--2025 (with races just after each test block left out, because form features carry their results). The maths is in \texttt{docs/methods.md} in the repository.

\textbf{Limits.} FIA grid penalties announced after qualifying are not parsed; driver mood, upgrades and team orders are invisible to the model; one race is a very small sample.}
